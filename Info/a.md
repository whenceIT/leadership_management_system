CASH MANAGEMENT ESSENTIALS
A Practical Guide for Branch, District and Provincial Managers
Whence Financial Services

1. Why This Guide Exists
Whence Financial Services has a policy of selecting managers from a pool of exiting Loan Consultants. As such, most branch managers were promoted from Loan Consultant roles, a policy that has been good for staff growth and morale. But it also means many managers running branches, and many of the district and provincial managers supervising them, did not train in finance. As a result, some of the best-performing managers on paper are still operating without a clear picture of the cash position of their branch.
This shows up in a familiar pattern, a branch is queried over poor performance, and the answer is “we are pushing” or “we shall continue pushing.” That answer is honest, but it is not useful, it does not identify which number went wrong, or what to do about it. The goal of this guide is to replace “we are pushing” with a specific, numbers-based answer: exactly which figure is off target, by how much, and what it means for the branch's ability to keep operating.
Every example in this guide uses a 10-workstation branch and round numbers so the arithmetic is easy to follow. The same logic applies at any branch size. Therefore, a branch can just substitute their own number of workstations.
2. The Numbers Every Manager Must Know
Before looking at examples, five numbers need to be second nature to every manager. These are the numbers the branch is judged on, whether or not a manager thinks about them day to day.
2.1 Minimum Loan Target
Each work station is expected to disburse a minimum of K40,000.00 per cycle. A branch's minimum loan target is set by the number of workstations the institution has allocated to it, not by headcount, and not by how many staff happen to be present.
Branch Minimum Loan Target = Number of Workstations × K40,000
2.2 Maximum Interest Rate
The highest applicable interest rate is 40% per cycle. This is the rate used in this guide to calculate the maximum amount a branch could ever expect back from what it disburses, the ceiling, not a promise.
Maximum Expected Repayment = Amount Disbursed × 1.40
2.3 Acceptable Default Level
By the end of each cycle, uncollected amounts (defaults) should fall below K5, 000.00, and at least 90% of collections should be full payments, not partial or negotiated ones (Re-loans). Anything worse than this is a warning sign, even if the loan target was met.
2.4 Mandatory Monthly Outflows
These payments leave the branch whether or not the branch is performing well, and must be planned for before any money is spent elsewhere (salaries, inter-branch transfers, or other expenses):
Mandatory Item	Monthly Amount
Branch debt repayment — no Loan Consultants recruited yet	K5,000
Branch debt repayment — Loan Consultants recruited	K10,000
Administration fee	K8,000
Building & Infrastructure fee	K10,000
Statutory payments	K14,500
Branch salaries & performance allowances	Varies by staff and targets met

Note: for a branch that has recruited Loan Consultants, the fixed, non-salary mandatory outflow is Admin (K8,000) + Building & Infrastructure (K10,000) + Statutory (K14,500) + Debt repayment (K10,000) = K42,500 per month, before a single Kwacha of salary is paid. This figure recurs throughout the examples below.
2.5 The Branch Cash Health Formula
Put together, these numbers give a single formula that tells a manager whether the branch is a going concern this cycle, or whether it is quietly eating into its own capital:
NET CASH POSITION =
  (Amount Disbursed × 1.40)
  − Defaults (uncollected amounts)
  − Mandatory Fixed Costs (Admin + Building + Statutory + Debt)
  − Salaries & Performance Allowances

•	Positive Net Cash Position: the branch generated more than it needed. Money can safely support new loans, or be sent to another branch, or set aside as a buffer.
•	Negative Net Cash Position: the branch spent more than it generated. Every month this continues, the branch is consuming capital it does not have, even if staff “worked hard” and even if the loan target was technically met.
The three worked examples below apply this formula to three different branches, all with 10 workstations, so the effect of each variable is easy to see in isolation.
3. Beyond the Mandatory Numbers: Irregular Operating Costs
The Net Cash Position formula in Section 2.5 accounts for the fixed, predictable monthly costs every branch carries. But a branch's real cash position also depends on a second layer of costs, costs that are certain to happen, but might not be certain to happen this month, and easy to overlook because they don't arrive as a tidy monthly bill. These include:
•	Office rent — all branches operate from rented premises, and rent is paid three (3) months in advance. This lands as one large lump sum every quarter, not a smooth monthly cost.
•	Motor vehicle repairs and servicing, which arise unpredictably and can be significant when they do.
•	Salary advances for Loan Consultants — normally up to K4,000, though unique circumstances can require more. These are deliberately available on demand, as a control against fraud and theft, so they cannot simply be scheduled away.
•	Recurring office consumables, such as printer toner cartridges.
•	Monthly petty cash.
•	Field expenses — for example, hiring light trucks or other suitable transport to seize pledged collateral that cannot fit into the branch's existing vehicles.
•	Staff welfare and incentives, such as birthday cakes and similar goodwill spending.
•	Funeral assistance — typically around K5,000 when a staff member loses a parent, rising to more than K10,000 when the loss is a staff member, their spouse, or a child.

None of these appear in the mandatory fixed-cost table in Section 2, because some are irregular rather than fixed monthly amounts, but they are just as real. A branch that spends or sends out whatever cash it happens to be holding, without first setting aside a reserve for costs like these, will hit a liquidity problem the moment one of them falls due, even in a cycle where the Net Cash Position looked healthy.
The Residual Cash Rule
Never treat gross collections, or even a positive Net Cash Position, as fully available to lend out or send elsewhere. Hold back a running reserve for irregular-but-certain costs, rent renewal, vehicle repairs, salary advances, and welfare or funeral support, before deciding what cash is truly free to move.

Managers who look only at collections, without this fuller picture, end up making cash movement decisions piecemeal, and mistaking gross collections for value the branch has actually created for the institution. Seeing the whole picture, not just the mandatory numbers, is what separates a branch that stays liquid from one that quietly runs into trouble.
4. Worked Example 1: The Under-Disbursing Branch
This is the branch that fails at the first number, it simply does not lend out enough.
Item	Amount
Workstations	10
Minimum loan target (10 × K40,000)	K400,000
Amount actually disbursed this cycle	K220,000
Maximum expected repayment (K220,000 × 1.40)	K308,000
Shortfall against loan target (K400,000 − K308,000)	K92,000

Even in the best possible case of say, 100% collection, zero defaults, and every client paying in full, this branch can only ever bring back K308,000 against a K400,000 target. The manager cannot close this gap by “pushing harder” on collections alone, the shortfall was locked in the day the branch under-disbursed.
Adding mandatory fixed costs makes the picture worse: K92,000 + K42,500 = K134,500 is the minimum this branch needs to recover from old defaults, refinancing, or other sources just to stand still this cycle, before salaries are even considered.
Verdict: Not a going concern (The branch is unlikely to continue existing into the foreseeable future)

The problem is disbursement, not effort. No amount of collection pressure fixes an under-lent branch. The manager needs to identify why disbursement fell short (staff capacity, client demand, risk appetite) and correct that directly.
5. Worked Example 2: The Healthy, On-Target Branch
This branch meets its loan target and keeps defaults under control — the combination the institution is aiming for.
Item	Amount
Workstations	10
Minimum loan target (10 × K40,000)	K400,000
Amount disbursed this cycle	K400,000
Maximum expected repayment (K400,000 × 1.40)	K560,000
Defaults (uncollected, below the K5,000 threshold)	K4,000
Actual amount collected (K560,000 − K4,000)	K556,000
Interest actually earned (K556,000 − K400,000 disbursed)	K156,000
Less: mandatory fixed costs	− K42,500
Less: salaries & allowances (illustrative, 10 staff)	− K100,000
Net Cash Position	K13,500

This branch is a going concern: it covers every mandatory cost, pays its staff, and still has a small surplus left over. Note how thin that margin is, K13,500 out of K400,000 disbursed. A small increase in defaults, or a poor month of disbursement, is enough to erase it. This is why even a healthy branch needs to be watched, not left alone.
Verdict: Going concern — but with a thin margin.
The branch meets the standard on both disbursement and collections. The manager's job now is to protect this margin: don't recruit or spend against it as if it were a large buffer, because it isn't one.
6. Worked Example 3: The “We Met Target” Branch That Is Still Losing Money
This is the branch most likely to be mistaken for healthy, because it hits the number managers usually get praised for: disbursement. What breaks it is collections.
Item	Amount
Workstations	10
Minimum loan target (10 × K40,000)	K400,000
Amount disbursed this cycle	K400,000
Maximum expected repayment (K400,000 × 1.40)	K560,000
Defaults (uncollected, far above the K5,000 threshold)	K60,000
Actual amount collected (K560,000 − K60,000)	K500,000
Interest actually earned (K500,000 − K400,000 disbursed)	K100,000
Less: mandatory fixed costs	− K42,500
Less: salaries & allowances (illustrative, 10 staff)	− K100,000
Net Cash Position	− K42,500

Disbursement was identical to the healthy branch in Example 2, same K400,000, same K560,000 ceiling. The only difference is K56,000 more in defaults. That single number turned a K13, 500 surplus into a K42, 500 deficit.
Verdict: Not a going concern, despite meeting the loan target.
This branch is eating its own capital exactly like Example 1, but for the opposite reason, collections, not disbursement. A manager who only checks “did we hit our loans target?” will miss this entirely. Defaults must be reviewed with the same seriousness as disbursement, every cycle.
